Before you share that explosive screenshot about a politician, run it through
this five-minute check. It will save you from spreading fakes — and from the
legal risk of repeating a false claim.

## 1. Find the original source

Ask: *where did this first appear?* A claim with no traceable origin — no
named outlet, no document, no on-record official — is a red flag. Real news
trails back to a primary source: a court order, an official statement, a named
report.

## 2. Reverse image search photos and screenshots

Old or unrelated photos are constantly recycled with new captions. Use a
reverse image search (Google Images, TinEye) to find where a picture *really*
came from and when it was first published.

## 3. Check the date

Outdated stories get re-shared as if they're new, especially around elections.
Always check the original publication date, not the date someone forwarded it.

## 4. Cross-check with independent sources

If only one anonymous account is saying it, be skeptical. Look for the same
fact reported by **multiple independent, credible outlets**, or confirm it
against a primary document. Indian fact-checkers (such as those certified by
the International Fact-Checking Network) often debunk viral claims quickly.

## 5. Separate fact from opinion and label status

- Is the claim **a fact** (verifiable) or **an opinion** (someone's view)?
- If it's about a case, is it an **allegation**, a **charge**, or a
  **conviction**? These are very different.

## The golden rule

If you can't link a credible source, don't present it as fact — and don't
share it. Verifying first protects your readers, your credibility, and you.
