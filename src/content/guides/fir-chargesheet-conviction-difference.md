When you read that a politician "has an FIR against them," it's easy to assume
the worst. But in India's legal system, an FIR is only the *beginning* of a
long process — and most stages are not findings of guilt. Here's what each term
means.

## 1. FIR (First Information Report)

An FIR is simply the **police's written record that a cognizable offence has
been reported**. Anyone can file one. It triggers an investigation. It is
**not** evidence, not a charge proven, and not a verdict. Many FIRs are later
closed for lack of evidence.

## 2. Investigation

The police gather evidence, record statements and examine documents. They may
arrest a person, but **arrest is not conviction** either — it is a step in
investigation, subject to bail and judicial review.

## 3. Charge sheet (final report)

If the police believe there is enough evidence, they file a **charge sheet**
before a court. This means the case is strong enough to go to trial — but it
still reflects the *prosecution's* claim, not a proven fact.

> A charge sheet says "we believe there is a case to answer," not "this person
> is guilty."

## 4. Framing of charges & trial

The court examines whether charges should be framed. A trial follows, where
both sides present evidence and the accused can defend themselves. Until the
trial ends, the person is **presumed innocent**.

## 5. Conviction or acquittal

Only a court can **convict** (find guilty) or **acquit** (find not guilty).
Convictions can also be appealed to higher courts. A conviction is the only
stage at which it is accurate to say someone "committed" the offence.

## Why this matters when you read the news

- "FIR filed" ≠ guilty.
- "Charge sheet filed" ≠ guilty.
- "Arrested" ≠ guilty.
- "Convicted" = found guilty by a court (and may still be under appeal).

Responsible reporting always states *which stage* a case is at. That's why on
this site every report carries a clear status label.
